# What Does a Crew Boss Actually Do on a Festival Build?

Most people think a crew boss turns up on the day with a hi-vis and a clipboard.

That's not what this job is.

I've been working builds for over a decade — Eurovision, the BAFTAs, BBC Radio 1 Big Weekend every year since 2016, Glastonbury, Tomorrowland, Formula E. I've stood on-site at 3am in Belgium waiting for a truck that's stuck at customs. I've re-drawn a scenic plan in my head because the ground conditions weren't what the drawings said. I've had to make calls that cost money either way, and I've had to make them fast.

A crew boss isn't a glorified attendance register. A crew boss is the person who keeps a build from unravelling.

Here's what actually happens.

---

## Before anyone steps foot on site

The job starts weeks before the build.

I'm reading through the technical drawings, flagging anything that doesn't match the spec sheet — because discrepancies between what the designer drew and what the production team ordered are more common than anyone likes to admit. I'm working out the crew call structure: who comes in first, what order the phases run in, where the chokepoints are going to be.

I'm checking certifications. On a complex build you might have IPAF-qualified operators on scissor lifts and boom lifts, CPCS card holders on plant, CSCS-certified carpenters doing scenic elements. Someone has to make sure the right people are assigned to the right tasks before day one, not discovered on day one.

I'm talking to the production manager about sequencing. If the rigging team needs to finish before the staging crew can advance, that dependency needs to be locked in. If it isn't, you lose half a day on a schedule that has no half days to lose.

---

## Site arrival

The first hour on a greenfield site tells you a lot.

Ground conditions, access points, where deliveries are actually going to land versus where the drawings say they'll land. I've been on sites where the production office was placed exactly where a truck route needed to be. I've been on sites where the power hookup was fifty metres further than anyone planned for.

None of this is unusual. What matters is how quickly you identify it and how you adapt.

I brief the crew before the tools come out. Not a five-minute chat — a proper brief. Who's responsible for what, what the schedule looks like, what the site rules are, where the welfare facilities are, what the escalation path is if something goes wrong. Crew who are clear on expectations work faster and make fewer mistakes. That's not management theory, that's just true.

---

## The build days

This is where the complexity lives.

On a large festival build — and I've worked sites running hundreds of crew across multiple stages — there are dozens of things moving at once. Structural steel going up on one end, scenic elements being pre-fabricated on another, plant operators moving ground equipment, carpenters fitting out production areas.

The crew boss is holding all of it in their head and watching for the moments when things are about to collide — when two teams need the same piece of ground at the same time, when a delivery lands that nobody's accounted for, when a task is running an hour behind and the next three tasks are dependent on it.

You make adjustments constantly. You don't wait for the production office to tell you there's a problem. By the time it reaches the production office, you've already lost time you didn't have.

Show days shift the dynamic completely. Now the build crew steps back and the event operates. But the crew boss is still accountable — for any structural or scenic element that needs attention, for any change that production calls during the event, for making sure the get-out starts clean.

---

## The get-out

The get-out is where a lot of the damage happens, and most of it is avoidable.

Crews are tired. The event's over. The instinct is to move fast and deal with the consequences later. But a disorganised get-out means equipment gets damaged, items get lost, the venue gets handed back in a state that costs someone money, and the relationship with the client suffers.

A good get-out is planned before the event opens. The crew knows the sequence, the transport is lined up, and the derig runs in the reverse order of the build — because that's usually the only order that doesn't create problems.

---

## What it costs to get this wrong

I'm not going to lecture anyone about safety. You know why it matters.

What I will say is that the less visible cost is time and relationship damage. A build that falls behind schedule because crew weren't briefed properly, or because no one caught the discrepancy in the drawings, or because the sequencing was wrong — that cost lands on the production manager and the client. It shows up as overtime, as rushed get-outs, as conversations nobody wants to have.

The crew boss is a risk management function as much as a logistics one. That's not how the role is usually sold, but it's what it actually does.
